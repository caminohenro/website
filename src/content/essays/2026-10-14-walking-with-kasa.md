In the Shikoku pilgrimage tradition, every *henro* walks with an invisible companion — Kōbō Daishi (弘法大師), the great monk who established the eighty-eight temple circuit twelve hundred years ago. Pilgrims inscribe their sedge hats with the phrase 同行二人 (*dōgyō ninin*): "two walking together." Even when the path is empty and the rain comes sideways, you are never alone.

I carry my own *kasa* now — a wide-brimmed woven hat that marks me as a pilgrim even here on the Iberian coast, thousands of kilometers from Shikoku. People stare. Children point. Old women in Galician villages cross themselves and then smile.

## The Weight of Companionship

There is a paradox in walking alone. The longer you walk, the more you realize how connected you are to everyone you meet. The baker who refuses payment for bread. The truck driver who pulls over to offer water. The old man on the bench who simply nods, as if to say: *I see you. Keep going.*

Each of these encounters is a kind of *osettai* — the freely given gift that sustains the pilgrim. In Japan, offering osettai to a henro is itself considered a spiritual practice. The giver receives as much as the pilgrim.

> "We do not walk alone. The path remembers everyone who has walked it before." — Shikoku proverb

## What the Hat Teaches

The *sugegasa* — the conical sedge hat — is more than rain protection. It is a symbol. When you wear it, you declare yourself a pilgrim. You are no longer a tourist, a traveler, a backpacker. You are someone on a path, moving toward something you cannot yet name.

The hat also teaches humility. It is cheap, fragile, and replaceable. It will not last the whole journey. Neither will my sandals, my pack, or my body as it is now. Everything on this walk is temporary. The walk itself is temporary. The only permanent thing is the willingness to keep walking.

## Bread and Figs

Today's provisions came from a small market in Cee: bread, cheese, dried figs, and oats. Total cost: €18.40, recorded faithfully in the [ledger](/finances). The figs are from Turkey — I smiled at the coincidence, since Turkey lies somewhere ahead on this road, months or years from now.

I ate sitting on a stone wall overlooking the estuary, watching fishing boats come in with the afternoon catch. A cat appeared and sat beside me, expecting nothing, offering nothing except the quiet company of another creature under the same sun.

This is what I came to find.

*— Henro, Cee, Spain, October 2026*
