## The Beggar

While walking the Camino, I initially imagined I would be doing a lot of begging. In fact, one of the reasons I thought it was such a good idea to don the white _henro_ outfit is that it would increase my pilgrim _ethos_ while begging. My first night after getting united with Kasa was spent at a _donativo_ albergue (which means they operate on a give-what-you-can basis). Since it was my first night, I had literally no money to give, and I felt ashamed. After talking with the volunteer there I decided to go into the nearby town and play my drum at the entrance of a local fair. That earned me a few coins which I left as my _donativo_.[1](#footnote-1)

As the days went by, I met other pilgrims and told many people my story. Sometimes (many times), I noticed a part of me fervently hoping that the story would result in a bit of money. That’s why I often felt torn about whether or not to include the part of the story where I started without money — I knew that something in me was wanting to include that as a sneaky way to manipulate people into giving, while another part was ashamed of that motivation and wanted to _only_ share that detail out of genuine excitement for the journey. So I felt like a **beggar** and was ashamed.

## The Salesman

Over time I learned to be more comfortable sharing that detail — especially when I _did_ have money, because I felt I didn’t especially need to share it, so I could make that choice totally out of a different energy. Partly, this comfort came from the realisation that, even if this person won’t give me money, eventually someone else will. So I started to share my story with more enthusiasm, and really believing in the power my “product”, instead of shaming myself for wanting money. I realized what I was doing was nothing short of **salesmanship**. Sure, I didn’t have a physical product, but I was selling a story. And everyone who works in marketing knows that it’s not the product that sells; it’s the story behind it. As for my story, some people would buy it, and others wouldn’t. But that’s just business.

#### The “Invisible Shop”

At one point I had this image that I was running an **“invisible shop**_**”**_: Just like a normal shop on the street, people walk by. The shop has a sign in front, with its name in big funny letters, to catch people’s attention. Some see it, enter and buy something. Others walk in, browse and don’t buy. Most people walk right past the shop without even noticing it. And then there’s people who already know the shop, or have heard of it, and go specifically there to buy something.

Now, me, I was walking around with this big funny hat and white vest, two long cane sticks and a big backpack, and often wearing sandals. That’s one hell of a store sign; it catches the eye. Everyday I had people wanting to take pictures. And whenever someone was curious enough to come talk to me, that’s like entering my shop — it’s my cue to start selling.[2](#footnote-2) So, I would tell my story, and _absolutely_ make a point of mentioning the part where I’m living off the road, mostly in the form of people’s generous support. And I would try to say this out of an energy of gratitude and plentifulness, where my focus is on how much I’ve received, rather than an energy of lack, or a fear of not having enough in the future (which would turn my words into a sneaky demand). That’s the same as a salesman saying how good the product is, not because they’re desperate to sell something, but because their focus is on how the product has already improved many other people’s lives.[3](#footnote-3)

## The Entrepreneur

On the Camino, every pilgrim carries a “pilgrim credential” (or “pilgrim passport”) where they collect stamps from the places they stayed in or visited. These stamps historically serve as proof that the person has completed the pilgrimage, but nowadays they’ve turned into a kind of game: pilgrims try to collect a lot of beautiful stamps, and shopkeepers tempt pilgrims into their shops by advertising they have stamps. Sometimes you’ll find people positioned on the trailside with a setup specifically to stamp pilgrims’ credentials with these beautiful wax stamps, reminiscent of pirates and the old times. But the stamps cost money to make, so they either charge a few euros or at least expect a donation in exchange for the stamp.

I got some inspiration from these people “selling” stamps: I had the idea to create my own stamp and use it a more sophisticated way to “beg”. The idea was simple: I would offer a really cool stamp to pilgrims as a special memento or souvenir, and invite them to contribute to my investment in creating the stamp. This was supposed to help alleviate the weight of shame — they were paying for the stamp, not for my well-being. The stamp would create more opportunities for me to receive money, so I saw it as an investment — thus I became an **entrepreneur**.

This wasn’t the first time I had invested, though. Already in ordering the _hakui_ (white vest), I saw it as an investment. Like I said before:

> _One of the reasons I thought it was such a good idea to don the white_ henro _outfit is that it would increase my pilgrim_ ethos _while begging._

In other words, the outfit was meant to increase my probability of receiving money or other forms of help. It, too, was an entrepreneurial move. I felt like a businessman making such investments.

#### The Business Model

I often joked with people that I had a “business model”. The model was this:

1.  I let people take my **picture;**
    
2.  I give them my **stamp;**
    
3.  I tell them my **story;**
    
4.  I get their **money**.
    

You might say it isn’t really a business model because I never actually ‘charge’ money for any of the three “products”. It is only in telling the story that there is an implicit invitation for people to give money. I give my picture and stamp because I enjoy it. I mention my needs and hope people will care enough to contribute willingly. If they don’t, that’s alright. I don’t expect everyone to give, because I trust that, from those who do give, I will get enough. After over a month on the road, this model holds true: I’ve always had enough, even when I had no money. Most days paid for themselves — that is, I received more money than the expenses I had. Rare was the day when I had to compensate with money received on a different day.

I don’t know if this will hold true after the Camino Francés, when there aren’t nearly as many pilgrims. But I’m confident that there will always be ways for me to get what I need, even if I have to change my strategies. That’s what this pilgrimage is all about: adapting, being creative, discovering what I can rely on, and creating my own opportunities — trusting also that God is on my side, providing opportunities that I can build on top of.

## The Beggar (again)

Despite all of the entrepreneurial “investments” and salesmanlike “pitches”, I sometimes still feel like a beggar. In fact, the lesson I took from all this is that there is basically no difference between these the beggar, the salesman and the entrepreneur. Every time we do something for money we are begging. There are more and less sophisticated ways of begging.

Most people work a regular job. That means they’re begging to only one person: their employer. They do all that work just so they can say, at the end of the month “please give me money”. What’s sad about that is that we forget we’re begging and, because we worked, we think we “deserve” the money, so we forget to be grateful for it and only remember to build resentment when the payment comes late, or when it is less than we want.

More ambitious people start their own companies. But that, too is a way of begging. We are essentially shifting our begging from one person (the boss) to multiple people (the customers) (notice how that’s what a classic beggar already does anyway: he begs to multiple people). Instead of using our labor as an excuse to demand money, we use a product.

Backpackers and travelers often use music, juggling, painting, and other forms of entretainment or art to beg for money on the road. This is called **busking**. It seems intuitively closer to “classic” begging because it’s still obvious that they’re asking for money, only they’re providing some kind of show to compensate you.

In reality, selling a product or service is also a kind of “show” people put up to add to their begging, the same way a busker does. Except, in this case, they _disguise_ their begging behind the product or service, so it doesn’t look like begging anymore. It looks instead like they’re doing _us_ a favor by selling, so we are the ones compensating them by paying.

Then, of course, there is _“classic”_ begging. This is the most humble form, because it strips our begging completely bare. We become nothing but a human being with an obvious need for sustenance. As we show that to others, we hope to be accepted for what we are, whereas in all other forms of begging, we are attempting to disguise our need for sustenance by replacing it with the narrative that we are doing something to _“deserve”_ it. Classic begging completely removes that and shows the naked truth: ultimately, I can never sustain myself alone — I am _always_ completely reliant on others, be it other men or natural creatures. Whatever product or service I’m selling, the product itself does not sustain me; it’s the people who buy it that do. If I forget that — if I trick myself into thinking that I (or my work) am the sole responsible for sustaining my life — I stop being connected with the actual source of life. I start to protect what I think of as “my money”, and forget that no money is never truly mine: it only belongs to the central bank which produced it, to which it will eventually return, and the rest is just a fleeting illusion of possession.

Of course, all “classic” beggars also use narratives to justify their needing the money — that’s their pitch. “I am a single mother to 3 kids”; “I lost my arms in the war”; “I have cancer”; “I am a pilgrim on the way to Japan”; most commonly, a simple “I need to eat”. In reality, nobody can ever beg without such a narrative. In other words, there is no begging without a product to sell. In classic begging, the product is the story.

---

As we close this circle, only one truth becomes apparent:

**We are all beggars** — and that is all we ever _can_ be, when it comes to seeking money.

Whether you humble yourself along the city streets, or busk your way across the country, or work a 9-to-5, or even build your own company… There’s no escaping the fact that all money always comes from someone else, and thus you are begging for money whenever you do something with the intention to receive it. It may be more obvious, it may be more sneaky; you may even disguise your begging behind a luxury product or service. And yet, at the same time…

**We are all salesmen** — there is no begging without a product, without a pitch, or without a narrative.

Whatever your source of income, you at some point had to convince someone to “buy your product”. Maybe it’s because you look pathetic enough, or crazy enough, or enlightened enough. Maybe it’s when you go on a job interview with a good resume, or when you have a good marketing team. Whatever it is, the person who gave you money has a reason to give it to you, and that reason is in the form of a narrative. _That narrative is your product._ Not the item you sold them.

**We are all entrepreneurs** — we all take risks and make investments in things we believe will help us make money.

It could be your studies, it could be your portfolio, it could be the industrial oven you bought to bake cookies to sell. It could even be that bus ticket which first took you to the big city where you found a big supermarket in front of which to humble yourself. Or it could be the white vest and stamp you decided to create before heading on a pilgrimage. All of these things are the investments you made to get where you are now, where you believed you would be most successful in whatever you intended to do.

---

Now that you know you are all of these three things, take a look at your life and notice where you’ve been holding back. Often times, what stands between us and a good opportunity is our judgement of one of these roles as being either beneath us, or above us.

-   **Don’t be afraid to beg** — you’re doing it anyway. Learn to ask for what you want, and to see a real gift in your requests to others.
    
-   **Don’t be ashamed to sell** — you’ve been doing it your whole life. Learn to see what you have to offer, and tell your story with genuine enthusiasm.
    
-   **Don’t hold back from investing** — you can’t go anywhere without taking risks. Invest in the things that lead to the future you want, and create your own opportunities to make it happen.
