### They say, a long time ago,

a pilgrim came from Japan to do the Camino de Santiago, in Spain. This was a japanese young man who had just recently completed the Shikoku Henro pilgrimage, in Japan.

According to legend, this man crossed into mainland Asia and then **walked** all the way to Finisterra. Just a little bit before Finisterra, there is a small village called Buxán. There, a refuge for pilgrims was built. It is called _Mi Mansion Perogrulla_. When our unnamed japanese pilgrim happened upon that refuge, he felt a strong connection to that place, as many pilgrims do. He spent some time there, bonded with the people and restored his strength to finish the last leg of the journey. And when he had finished the journey, he returned to Buxán to leave a parting gift: his conical straw hat, from the Shikoku pilgrimage. The gift was meant to symbolize his connection to that place and the end of his long pilgrimage. It was worn from the journey and had a hole on the top. Still, its story was worth preserving, so the hat was hung from a wall at _Mi Mansion Perogrulla_ for several years, displaying its mysterious beauty to all pilgrims who passed by.

### One day, years later,

on a fine April afternoon, a portuguese pilgrim called Vasco passed by _Mi Mansion Perogrulla_ on his way to Finisterra. One among many, there was nothing very special about this pilgrim — except a hidden intention. You see, this pilgrim was preparing for something bigger. He, too, was planning to walk across the landmass, but was still too afraid to say it out loud. In fact, that was the reason he was there: to test his grit on a smaller pilgrimage, from Portugal to Finisterra. When a new asian volunteer at the refuge donned the hat for fun, and it looked good on her, an older volunteer told the story of its origins within Vasco’s earshot. Immediately when he heard about someone crossing from Japan to Finisterra on foot, his eyes began to glow. Knowing that someone had done it before was the inspiration he needed to muster the confidence to go on his own journey. To him, the hat became a symbol for what was possible.

### Later on,

after returning home from Finisterra, as Vasco was planning his big journey, he decided to research what ancient japanese pilgrims had historically worn on their travels. That’s when he first found out about Shikoku Henro and learned about the traditional _henro_ attire, which included a _kasa_, a conical straw hat just like the one at the refuge. Once he had connection, an intention was formed that was clear as water: he would return to Buxán to request the hat, and bring it back home. But he could not simply take the hat: he had to take on the role of a _henro_ — that is, a pilgrim of the Shikoku pilgrimage — and wear the rest of the attire as well. So he ordered a _hakui_, the white vest, and then he set out to Buxán to see about the hat.

On the way to Fisterra, Vasco knew he had to let go of his old persona in order to create room in his mind to become who the camino required him to be. He had to take on a new identity, one that could summon the necessary strength to tackle this long pilgrimage. He thought long and hard about what name to use… and when he finally arrived in Buxán wearing his white vest, it came to him. Something truly clicked when the word “Henro" happened upon his mind. It was the only correct name. But Henro’s identity was undoubtedly bound by Kasa, for it was the hat who gave him a purpose, a mission. Kasa was to Henro just like the _One Ring_ was to Frodo.

### And so it begun,

the journey of Henro, when after two days of back-and-forth at the refuge, permission was finally granted to take the hat, Kasa, on his way to Japan.

Now Henro is determined to keep going, uniting the two pilgrimages of Santiago and Shikoku as his predecessor once did, with a mission to return Kasa to its original home in Japan, leaving him to rest at the **88th and** **final temple** of the Shikoku island as a symbolic gesture and closing of the circle.
